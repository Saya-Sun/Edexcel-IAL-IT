Archiving Data

Understand how organisations securely preserve historical data and why archiving supports regulatory compliance, business continuity, historical analysis, auditing and system migrations.

📚 Using IT Systems in Organisations📝 Detailed Lesson Notes
Lesson overview: This lesson explains the purpose of data archiving, the five textbook reasons for retaining old records, and practical ways archives support businesses. It also covers the process, benefits, challenges and an online shopping case study.
📚
Section 01

Introduction to Data Archiving

Organisations generate and store large amounts of data through their daily activities, including customer records, financial transactions, employee information, sales records and emails. However, not all data needs to be accessed equally often.

Some information is used continuously, while other information becomes less important for daily operations as it gets older. For example, a supermarket may regularly access today's sales but rarely need to look at sales records from five years ago.

Keeping all old records in the primary operational system can increase storage requirements and make the system harder to manage. In some systems, it may also reduce performance. Organisations therefore use data archiving.

📌 Key definition — Data archiving

Data archiving is the process of storing historical data that is no longer frequently used in a secure location so that it remains accessible when required. It preserves important information while reducing the volume of inactive data in primary operational systems.

1.1 What is historical data?

Historical data is information collected or created in the past. Even though it may no longer be needed for everyday activities, it can still be useful.

💳 Financial records

Previous years' sales transactions, invoices and financial statements.

👥 Customer and staff records

Completed customer orders, former employee details and old correspondence.

🏥 Service records

Historical patient records or records of completed appointments and services.

📁 Project records

Reports, documents and data from projects that have already finished.

1.2 Primary operational storage vs archive storage

In this lesson, primary operational storage means the main system used for current business activities. Archive storage is used to preserve older or inactive records for occasional use.

Primary operational storage Archive storage
Stores frequently accessed data Stores less frequently accessed historical data
Supports current daily operations Supports long-term retention and future retrieval
Often needs fast access Access speed may be less important
Contains mainly active records Contains mainly inactive or older records
May use high-performance storage May use lower-cost storage where appropriate

1.3 Example: A bank

A bank processes millions of transactions. Recent transactions are needed for everyday banking, but transactions from several years ago may be accessed only occasionally.

The bank can move selected older transactions into a secure archive. These records remain retrievable for customer enquiries, financial investigations, legal requirements and audits, while the primary transaction database carries less inactive data.

2.1 Compliance and Regulatory Requirements

What is compliance?

Compliance means following the laws, regulations, standards and policies that apply to an organisation. Different sectors may be required to retain particular records for specific periods, even after customers no longer use their services.

Banking, healthcare, education and insurance organisations, for example, may have legal duties to preserve certain records.

Why is archiving important for compliance?

  1. Meeting legal obligations: Important records can be retained for the time required by applicable laws.
  2. Providing evidence: Organisations can retrieve records to demonstrate that relevant rules were followed.
  3. Reducing pressure on primary systems: Older records can be stored away from everyday operational data.
  4. Protecting information: Security and access controls reduce the risk of unauthorised access or modification.
  5. Managing retention: Record-retention policies specify when information should be preserved and when it may be deleted.
💡 Example — A financial institution

A bank may have to keep transaction records even after a customer closes an account. Secure archiving allows the bank to retrieve these transactions for audits or investigations without leaving every historical record in its main processing database.

⚠️ Important

Retention laws differ between countries and industries. Records should not automatically be stored forever: they must also be disposed of securely when retention is no longer required or justified.

🛡️

2.2 Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery

What is business continuity?

Business continuity is an organisation's ability to continue essential activities during and after a disruption. Possible disruptions include hardware and software failures, fire, flooding, power cuts, cyberattacks, accidental deletion and natural disasters.

What is disaster recovery?

Disaster recovery is the process of restoring systems, services and data following a serious incident. It helps an organisation return to normal operations.

How can archives help?

  • Preservation: Important historical information remains stored separately from the main system.
  • Recovery support: Properly protected archived copies may be retrieved after some failures or disasters.
  • Reduced disruption: Staff can still obtain older documents that are needed for essential tasks.
  • Protection: Independently protected or replicated archives can reduce the impact of certain incidents.
  • Organisational knowledge: Historical records can remain available if an original system is lost.
💡 Example — A hospital

A hospital keeps older patient histories in secure archive storage. If its main database fails, an independently accessible archive may still provide useful historical medical information. However, the hospital also needs separate backups of its current patient records and a disaster recovery plan.

Data archiving vs data backup

Data archiving Data backup
Preserves historical records for future access Creates copies of data so it can be restored
Usually focuses on older, less-used data Protects current and active data as well
May move records away from primary storage Normally retains the original operational data
Supports retention, compliance and analysis Supports recovery from deletion, corruption or failure
⚠️ Remember

Archiving is not a replacement for backups. Archiving preserves historical records; backups and disaster recovery arrangements protect and restore systems and data.

2.3 Historical Analysis and Reporting

What is historical analysis?

Historical analysis involves examining previously collected data to discover trends, patterns and changes over time. It helps organisations understand previous performance and improve future plans.

Businesses may compare annual sales, study customer purchasing habits, identify seasonal demand, review operating costs, examine staff performance or investigate previous transport and production activity.

How does archived data help?

Archived data provides information from earlier months or years. Managers can compare it with current results to identify long-term trends, predict demand, plan budgets and make more informed decisions.

💡 Example — A supermarket

A supermarket retrieves archived annual sales figures to compare results across four years.

Connection with Management Information Systems (MIS)

A Management Information System (MIS) gathers and processes information to support management decisions. An MIS can retrieve archived sales information, combine it with recent data and produce comparison reports.

  • Compare current and past performance.
  • Forecast future demand.
  • Plan budgets and resources.
  • Make evidence-based decisions.
💡 Key idea

Archived information is not merely stored and forgotten: it may still be valuable for business intelligence and long-term planning.

2.4 Auditors and Compliance Audits

What is an audit?

An audit is a systematic examination of records, systems, activities or processes to check their accuracy and whether required rules or standards have been followed. It may be carried out by internal auditors, external auditors or regulatory bodies.

How does archiving support audits?

Auditors frequently need records relating to activities that happened months or years earlier. Archived data can preserve evidence of financial transactions, business activities, employee actions, communication, compliance and changes to important records.

  1. Past-activity evidence: Allows auditors to establish what happened.
  2. Transaction verification: Helps compare historic invoices and payments with financial statements.
  3. Compliance evidence: Supports demonstrations that rules and procedures were followed.
  4. Investigation: Helps investigate errors, suspected fraud or misconduct.
  5. Accountability: Supports checks on which actions were performed and when.
💡 Example — Financial audit

An external auditor examines a company's accounts and requests invoices, purchase orders and payment records from three years earlier. The company retrieves them from the archive so the auditor can verify recorded transactions against supporting documents.

Data integrity and security

Data integrity means preserving the accuracy, consistency and completeness of information. Archived records must be reliable if they are to be used as audit evidence.

🔐 Access permissions

Restrict who can view or change archived records.

🔒 Encryption

Protect sensitive archived information against unauthorised reading.

🧾 Audit logs

Record significant access and actions taken in the archive.

✅ Integrity checks

Help detect errors or unauthorised changes to stored data.

An audit trail is a chronological record of system activities or changes, such as who accessed a record and when.

🔄

2.5 Legacy Systems and Migrations

What is a legacy system?

A legacy system is an older computer system or application that an organisation continues to use, even if newer alternatives exist. It may depend on outdated software, older hardware, uncommon file formats or technologies that are difficult to maintain.

What is system migration?

System migration means moving data, applications or services from one system or platform to another. For instance, a business may replace an older customer database with a modern cloud-based application.

Why does archiving help?

  1. Preserves legacy information: Old records can remain available after a system is replaced.
  2. Supports continuity: Employees can still retrieve historical information.
  3. Reduces migration complexity: Not every inactive record must be placed in the new operational database.
  4. Protects data integrity: Verification and validation help maintain reliable records during transfer.
  5. Reduces reliance on old technology: Preserving data in accessible formats can help organisations retire outdated systems safely.
💡 Example — A school changing systems

A school replaces its 15-year-old student management system. It transfers current students' details into the new application but securely archives former students' records that must still be retained. Staff can retrieve authorised historical records without storing all of them in the new active database.

Section 03 · Additional explanation

How Data Archiving Works

The textbook mainly explains why organisations archive data. These six steps show a typical practical approach to archiving.

  1. Identify data to archive

    Review the organisation's records and select information no longer needed frequently. For example, older sales transactions may be candidates for archiving.

  2. Set retention and access rules

    Decide how long records must be stored and which staff or systems should be allowed to retrieve them.

  3. Choose appropriate archive storage

    Select a suitable solution, such as dedicated servers, network-attached storage or cloud archival services, based on cost, security and retrieval needs.

  4. Transfer and verify data

    Copy or move selected records securely, then confirm that the archived information is complete, accurate and retrievable before removing any original records.

  5. Secure and manage the archive

    Use safeguards such as access controls, encryption, integrity checks and appropriate backup or replication arrangements.

  6. Retrieve or dispose of records when appropriate

    Let authorised users retrieve archived information when required. Securely delete records once relevant retention obligations and policies permit disposal.

⚠️ Important

An archive should be tested for successful retrieval. Storing records is of little use if the organisation cannot open and understand them later.

Section 04 · Additional explanation

Advantages and Challenges

4.1 Advantages of data archiving

Advantage Explanation
Improved performance Can reduce the volume of inactive data handled by primary systems.
Lower primary storage requirements Moves historical records away from everyday operational storage.
Regulatory compliance Helps retain information for mandatory retention periods.
Preservation of records Keeps important historical information accessible.
Historical analysis Supports long-term reporting and decision-making.
Audit support Provides evidence of past activities and transactions.
Migration support Preserves useful information when IT systems change.

4.2 Challenges and limitations

💰 Storage and maintenance costs

Archives still require suitable storage, security, monitoring and maintenance.

⏳ Slower retrieval

Older information may take longer to access than data in the active system.

🔐 Security risks

Sensitive archived information must be protected from unauthorised access or cyberattacks.

🧩 Compatibility problems

Old software and file formats may become difficult to use as technology changes.

✅ Integrity concerns

Records may be damaged, incomplete or corrupted without appropriate checks.

📅 Retention mistakes

Early deletion may break legal obligations, while excessive retention may cause privacy risks.

Section 07

Lesson Summary

Data archiving allows organisations to preserve historical information securely while keeping it available when needed. It can also reduce the amount of inactive data stored in operational systems.

The five main textbook reasons for archiving are:

  1. Compliance and regulatory requirements: Retain records for legally required periods and demonstrate compliance.
  2. Business continuity and disaster recovery: Protect and preserve historical information so it can support recovery arrangements.
  3. Historical analysis and reporting: Use past information to identify trends and support decisions.
  4. Auditors and compliance audits: Provide trustworthy evidence of previous transactions and activities.
  5. Legacy systems and migrations: Keep old records accessible when systems are upgraded or replaced.
⭐ Most important idea

Data can become less useful in everyday operations without losing its long-term value. Archiving preserves that value while helping organisations manage their active systems efficiently.
