Disaster Recovery Plans

Explore how organisations protect critical data, prepare for serious IT disruptions and restore essential services through effective disaster recovery planning.

📚 Using IT Systems in Organisations📝 Detailed Lesson Notes
Lesson overview: Organisations depend on IT systems for their daily activities. Hardware failures, cyberattacks, natural disasters, human errors and power failures can interrupt these systems, risking data loss, financial harm and extended downtime.

A disaster recovery plan (DRP) prepares the organisation to restore vital IT services and maintain business continuity. This lesson covers key data, RTO and RPO, backups, risk analysis, team responsibilities, plan approval, maintenance and documentation.

🛡️
Section 01

Understanding Disaster Recovery Plans

1.1 What is a Disaster Recovery Plan?

Definition – Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP)

A documented set of procedures and strategies explaining how an organisation will recover critical IT systems, restore important data and resume operations after a serious disruption or disaster.

A disaster recovery plan forms an important part of an organisation's IT governance and policies. It should identify critical systems, possible threats, backup methods, recovery priorities, team responsibilities and communication arrangements.

Possible incidents include:

  • Hardware failures, such as a damaged server.
  • Cyberattacks, including ransomware.
  • Natural disasters, such as floods, fires and earthquakes.
  • Human error, such as accidental deletion of files.
  • Power failures and network interruptions.
  • Malicious actions by internal or external attackers.

1.2 Main Objectives of a DRP

⏱️

Minimise downtime

Restore important systems as quickly as possible so that service interruptions are reduced.

🔐

Protect data

Use backups and recovery procedures to prevent or minimise permanent data loss.

🏢

Maintain business continuity

Keep essential organisational activities running or resume them promptly after a disruption.

Key term – Downtime

The period during which an IT service or system is unavailable or unable to operate normally.

✅ Example – School online learning platform

A school's main server fails, making student records, exam results and lessons unavailable. Without a DRP, employees may not know which data to recover or who is responsible. With a DRP, the IT team can follow a planned sequence to restore data from backups and resume critical services.

💡 Remember

A DRP is not just a backup schedule. It also covers recovery objectives, risk analysis, responsibilities, communication, testing and management approval.

Section 02

Key Data

Disaster recovery must focus on the data and systems that an organisation cannot afford to lose. Not all information has equal importance: losing a temporary file may cause little inconvenience, while losing financial records or a patient database may have serious consequences.

2.1 Inventory of Data

Definition – Inventory of Data

A detailed record identifying the critical data and IT systems covered by the disaster recovery plan.

An inventory can record the name of the data or system, its purpose, storage location, responsible person, importance and backup requirements.

School data/system Purpose Importance
Student database Student and enrolment records Critical
Examination results Grades and assessment records Critical
Learning platform Online lessons and resources High
Staff records Employee information High
Old promotional materials Previous advertising material Low

The inventory helps identify recovery priorities, prevents important data being overlooked and supports the choice of suitable backup procedures.

2.2 Recovery Objectives

After identifying critical systems, the organisation must decide how quickly a system should be restored and how much recent data loss is acceptable.

⏱️ Recovery Time Objective (RTO)

The maximum target time that an affected service should remain unavailable before it is restored.

Question: How quickly must the system work again?

💾 Recovery Point Objective (RPO)

The maximum acceptable amount of recent data loss, measured in time, following a disruption.

Question: How much recent data could be lost?

A. Recovery Time Objective (RTO)

Suppose a bank sets an RTO of 30 minutes for a transaction-processing system. The organisation aims to restore that service within 30 minutes after a disruption. More critical services generally require shorter RTOs.

Illustration – RTO of 4 hours

9:00 AMSystem fails
1:00 PMTarget recovery deadline
RTO = 4 hours
If failure occurs at 9:00 AM, the service should be restored by 1:00 PM to meet this target.

A very short RTO may require standby servers, redundant infrastructure, specialist staff or other additional resources.

B. Recovery Point Objective (RPO)

Suppose a business has an RPO of one hour for its customer database. After a failure, the database should be recoverable to a point no more than one hour before the incident. Hourly recoverable backups might support this target, provided they complete reliably.

Illustration – RPO of 1 hour

9:00 AMEarlier data
11:00 AMSystem fails
Latest usable backup: 10:00 AM
The period from 10:00 to 11:00 AM represents up to one hour of changes that might be lost.

Systems with rapidly changing or highly valuable data may need a short RPO. Less frequently updated archives may tolerate a longer RPO.

C. Comparing RTO and RPO

Feature RTO RPO
Full name Recovery Time Objective Recovery Point Objective
Main focus System downtime Data loss
Question How fast must the system be restored? How much recent data can be lost?
Example Restore within 2 hours Lose no more than 30 minutes of data
Primary concern Availability of a service Recoverability of information
⭐ Important distinction

A system might recover quickly but still lose substantial data; it might also recover nearly all its data but take too long to resume service. A DRP needs both RTO and RPO.

2.3 Backup and Recovery Procedures

Definition – Data Backup

A separate copy of important data that can be used to restore information if the original is lost, corrupted or damaged.

The DRP should explain exactly how data is backed up, stored, protected and restored.

📥 Backup procedures

  • Identify what must be backed up.
  • Set backup frequency according to RPO.
  • Choose storage locations, including off-site options.
  • Protect backups with encryption and access controls.
  • Assign responsibility for monitoring backups.

♻️ Recovery procedures

  1. Identify damaged or unavailable systems.
  2. Locate a safe, suitable backup.
  3. Prepare the recovery environment.
  4. Restore data and applications.
  5. Verify accuracy and functionality.
  6. Return services to authorised users.
✅ Example – Online shop

An online shop backs up its order database every 30 minutes. A cyberattack damages the database at 3:00 PM. The team selects an unaffected backup, restores it and checks the recovered orders. If the latest usable backup is from 2:30 PM, up to 30 minutes of recent orders may be missing unless another recovery method is available.

Section 03

Risk Analysis

Definition – Risk Analysis

The process of identifying potential threats to data and IT systems, assessing their possible consequences and developing strategies to reduce the likelihood or impact of those threats.

Risk analysis considers threats in relation to the organisation's core functions. It has three main stages:

1. Threat IdentificationWhat could go wrong?
2. Impact AssessmentWhat damage might it cause?
3. Risk MitigationHow can the risk be reduced?

3.1 Threat Identification

Definition – Threat Identification

Recognising and recording specific events or activities that could damage, interrupt or compromise organisational data and IT systems.

A nominated person, often the senior IT manager, should carry out a comprehensive assessment of potential threats. Threats may be internal or external.

🏢 Internal threats

Threats originating within the organisation.

  • Accidental deletion of data.
  • Malicious employee activity.
  • Incorrect system configuration.
  • Poor equipment maintenance.
  • Inappropriate access permissions.

🌍 External threats

Threats originating outside the organisation.

  • Cyberattacks and ransomware.
  • Floods and earthquakes.
  • Fire affecting the premises.
  • Power supply failure.
  • External cloud or network outages.
💡 Be specific

“IT failure” is a vague threat. “Flood damage to the main server room” is specific and makes it easier to plan a suitable response, such as relocating equipment or maintaining backups at another site.

3.2 Impact Assessment

Definition – Impact Assessment

Evaluating the possible consequences of each identified threat for data, IT systems and business operations.

📁

Impact on data

Information might be lost, corrupted or exposed to unauthorised users.

🖥️

Impact on IT systems

Services might be unavailable, unreliable or expensive to restore.

📉

Impact on operations

Employees or customers may be affected, leading to delays, costs or reputational damage.

✅ Example – Hospital system

If a hospital's patient information system becomes unavailable, clinicians may struggle to view patient histories or test results. This may delay treatment, disrupt appointments and threaten patient safety. Because the potential impact is high, the system may require very short recovery objectives.

Impact assessments also help determine appropriate RTOs and RPOs. Critical services should usually be given higher recovery priority.

System Potential impact Illustrative priority
Emergency patient records Very high Very short RTO and RPO
Online payment system High Short RTO and RPO
Internal notice board Low Longer RTO may be acceptable
Historical advertising archive Very low Lower recovery priority

3.3 Risk Mitigation Strategies

Definition – Risk Mitigation

Measures that reduce the likelihood of a threat occurring or minimise the damage if it occurs.

Risk mitigation cannot guarantee that a threat will never occur. An effective strategy must consider prevention and reducing the consequences.

Threat Reduce likelihood Reduce impact
Cyberattack Firewalls, updates, staff security training Isolated backups and incident recovery
Hardware failure Regular maintenance and monitoring Spare equipment and redundant systems
Fire Fire detection and prevention measures Off-site backups and alternative facilities
Human error Training and restricted access Backups and version recovery
Power failure Electrical infrastructure maintenance UPS and backup generators
Flooding Avoid high-risk equipment locations Geographically separate recovery facilities

Prevention vs recovery: A firewall may reduce the likelihood of an attack. Restoring a clean backup after an attack is a recovery measure. Both matter.

Section 04

Team Actions

A DRP is effective only if the people involved know their responsibilities. A large organisation may have a dedicated disaster recovery team; a smaller organisation may assign the work to its usual IT support staff.

4.1 Defining Team Roles

The plan should identify who performs each task, when it should be done and who takes over if the main person is unavailable.

Possible team member Main responsibility
Disaster Recovery Manager Coordinates recovery and major decisions
IT Infrastructure Technician Repairs or replaces hardware and restores servers
Database Administrator Recovers databases and validates restored data
Network Administrator Restores connections and network services
Security Officer Investigates security incidents and protects restored systems
Communications Officer Updates employees, management and stakeholders

These are examples of roles; the exact structure depends on the organisation. Clear responsibility prevents confusion, duplicated tasks, overlooked activities and unnecessary delays.

✅ Example – Replacement personnel

If the database administrator is unavailable when a server fails, the plan should name a suitably trained replacement who can follow the documented restoration instructions.

4.2 Communication Plan

Definition – Communication Plan

A documented procedure explaining how relevant people will receive information during and after a disaster.

A communication plan should state who to contact, who sends updates, what information to share, which communication channels to use and when updates are due.

📣 Internal communication

  • Notify senior management.
  • Tell staff which services are unavailable.
  • Assign tasks to IT team members.
  • Update department managers on progress.

📨 External communication

  • Notify customers of interruptions.
  • Contact external IT service providers.
  • Inform affected suppliers.
  • Contact relevant authorities when necessary.
✅ Example – School communication

If an online learning platform fails, the IT manager informs the principal; teachers receive instructions through an alternative channel; students and parents are told about changes to lessons; and the IT team provides status updates.

💡 Remember

Communication plans need current contact details and backup communication methods. The organisation's normal email or messaging system may be unavailable during the incident.

4.3 Training and Drills

🎓 Training

Employees learn their responsibilities, how to follow recovery instructions, how to restore data and how to communicate during a disaster.

🧪 Drills

Planned exercises allow employees to practise the DRP and identify weaknesses before a real incident occurs.

Definition – Disaster Recovery Drill

A practice exercise used to test the effectiveness of disaster recovery procedures.

Example drill – Simulated server failure

  1. Simulate a server failure in a safe test environment.
  2. Activate the appropriate recovery procedures.
  3. Restore the test system from a backup.
  4. Measure the time required for recovery.
  5. Check that all necessary data is present and accurate.
  6. Record weaknesses and improve the plan.

Regular training and drills can reduce confusion, improve coordination and show whether recovery objectives can realistically be achieved.

Documentation: Records of training, drills, identified problems and corrective actions should be kept with the DRP. They can provide useful evidence during reviews or audits.

Section 05

Management of Disaster Recovery Plans

A disaster recovery plan must not become an outdated document that nobody reads. Organisations should manage the plan continuously so it reflects new technologies, business requirements, employees and threats.

5.1 Plan Approval

Definition – Plan Approval

The formal process in which senior management reviews and authorises a disaster recovery plan.

After the disaster recovery team develops the plan, senior management should approve it and commit to providing the necessary resources.

✅

Official authorisation

Confirms that the organisation accepts the recovery strategy.

💰

Necessary resources

Supports spending on equipment, backups, facilities and training.

📌

Accountability

Clarifies responsibility for implementing and supporting the plan.

✅ Example – School backup server

A school's IT department proposes an extra server and off-site backups. Senior management must approve the plan and provide the required budget and resources.

5.2 Plan Maintenance

Definition – Plan Maintenance

The ongoing process of reviewing and updating the DRP to keep it accurate, relevant and effective.

A review may be required when hardware is replaced, databases are moved, employees change roles, new services are introduced or new cybersecurity threats emerge. Any updated plan should be sent to senior management for approval.

  1. Review the existing plan

    Check whether procedures still meet operational needs.

  2. Identify required changes

    Consider new systems, staff, business activities and threats.

  3. Update recovery procedures

    Revise backup arrangements, responsibilities and technical instructions.

  4. Obtain management approval

    Submit the updated plan for formal authorisation.

  5. Communicate and test changes

    Ensure staff understand the new procedures and can use them successfully.

✅ Example – Moving to cloud services

A business transfers its customer database from a physical server to a cloud platform. Its old DRP still describes restoring the physical server. The organisation needs to rewrite the recovery steps for the new environment and obtain approval for the revised plan.

5.3 Documentation

Definition – Disaster Recovery Documentation

The written records, recovery instructions and technical information required to protect and restore an organisation's IT systems and data.

Document Purpose
Disaster recovery plan Overall procedures, priorities and responsibilities
Data inventory Identifies critical data and systems
Backup procedures Explains backup creation, storage and protection
Recovery instructions Step-by-step system restoration guidance
Network diagrams Shows devices and connections
Configuration records Records important technical settings
Contact lists Provides contact details for key people
Training and drill records Records tests, issues and improvements
⚠️ Essential requirement

Documentation should be clear, accurate and easily accessible to authorised staff. It should also be stored in a separate location or system that will remain available if the main IT infrastructure fails.

For example, if the only copy of recovery instructions is stored on the server that has failed, the team may be unable to access the instructions. Protected off-site copies can prevent this problem.

Section 06

Practical Example: Online Learning Platform

📚 Scenario

A school operates a platform for 500 students and 30 teachers. It stores student registrations, lesson materials, examination results, assignment submissions and account information. A serious server failure makes the platform unavailable.

Step 1 – Identify Key Data and Recovery Targets

Data / system Importance Example RTO Example RPO
Student database Critical 2 hours 30 minutes
Examination results Critical 2 hours 1 hour
Assignment submissions High 4 hours 1 hour
Lesson materials High 8 hours 24 hours
Archived announcements Low 48 hours 24 hours

These RTO and RPO values are illustrative teaching examples, not universal requirements.

Step 2 – Analyse Risks

Threat Possible impact Mitigation strategy
Server failure Platform unavailable Backups and alternative server resources
Ransomware Data may be inaccessible Security controls and protected backups
Accidental deletion Loss of student work Access controls and versioned backups
Power failure Services stop operating UPS and alternative power arrangements

Step 3 – Assign Responsibilities

  • IT Manager: coordinates the disaster recovery process.
  • System Administrator: restores servers and applications.
  • Database Administrator: restores and verifies student data.
  • School Management: approves resources and important decisions.
  • Communications Officer: informs teachers, students and parents.

Step 4 – Follow the Recovery Procedure

  1. 10:00 AM – Detect the failure

    Monitoring identifies that the main server has stopped responding.

  2. 10:10 AM – Notify the team

    The IT manager activates the plan and alerts responsible staff.

  3. 10:20 AM – Assess the damage

    The team determines which services and data are affected.

  4. 10:30 AM – Start restoration

    A replacement environment is prepared and clean backups are selected.

  5. 11:30 AM – Verify the recovery

    Data, accounts and important application functions are tested.

  6. 11:45 AM – Resume critical services

    The student database and essential learning functions become available again.

⭐ Applying RTO and RPO

In this example, essential services are restored in 1 hour 45 minutes, within a two-hour RTO. The team must separately check that the restored data also meets the agreed RPOs.

Step 5 – Review and Improve

After the incident, the school should investigate its cause, measure recovery performance, check for data loss, evaluate staff communication and update the plan wherever weaknesses are discovered. Significant updates should be formally approved.

Section 07

Advantages and Limitations

7.1 Advantages of a Disaster Recovery Plan

Advantage Explanation
Reduced downtime Prepared procedures can help restore important services faster.
Reduced data loss Backups and recovery methods support restoration of important records.
Business continuity Essential activities can continue or resume after a disruption.
Clear responsibilities Employees know who must complete each task.
Better communication Stakeholders receive organised and timely updates.
Reduced financial damage Faster recovery may reduce lost revenue and extra operational costs.
Improved preparedness Training and drills help people respond more effectively.

7.2 Limitations of a Disaster Recovery Plan

Limitation Explanation
Implementation costs Additional hardware, storage and facilities can be expensive.
Maintenance requirements The plan must be reviewed when technology, staff or threats change.
Training costs Employees need time and resources to practise recovery procedures.
No complete protection Not every disaster can be predicted or prevented.
Possible recovery failures Backups may be corrupted, missing or inaccessible.
Dependence on staff Recovery may be delayed if trained personnel are unavailable.

Overall: A DRP reduces risk and improves preparedness, but it cannot guarantee zero downtime or zero data loss.

Essential Terms

Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP)

Documented procedures to restore critical IT systems and data after a disruption.

Downtime

The period when a service is unavailable.

Business continuity

The ability to maintain or resume essential operations.

Data inventory

A record of important information and systems needing protection.

RTO

The target maximum period for recovering a system.

RPO

The maximum acceptable data loss measured in time.

Backup

A separate copy of data that can be used for restoration.

Risk analysis

Identifying threats, assessing consequences and planning mitigation.

Threat identification

Recognising possible sources of IT system or data damage.

Impact assessment

Evaluating how an incident could affect data, systems and operations.

Risk mitigation

Reducing a threat's likelihood or consequences.

Recovery drill

A practice exercise to test disaster recovery procedures.

Plan maintenance

Reviewing and updating the DRP to keep it effective.

Plan approval

Formal authorisation by senior management.

✅ Final understanding

Effective disaster recovery requires preparation before a disaster occurs. Organisations should identify critical systems, decide suitable recovery targets, assess risks, assign responsibilities, train staff and regularly update an approved recovery plan. This can minimise downtime, protect data and support business continuity.
