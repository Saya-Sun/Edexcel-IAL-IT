IT Governance and Policy

Understand how organisations direct and control IT resources, manage risks, protect information, and use business continuity, disaster recovery and acceptable use policies to maintain effective operations.

📚 Using IT Systems in Organisations📝 Detailed Lesson Notes
Lesson overview: Learn the five goals of IT governance and explore the four important policies: business continuity, disaster recovery, risk management and acceptable use. Includes examples, comparisons and a school IT risk assessment activity.
🏛️
Section 01

Understanding IT Governance

Every organisation depends on information technology (IT) for tasks such as storing information, communicating with customers, processing transactions and making decisions. However, IT also creates risks: cyberattacks, system failures, data loss, misuse of resources and non-compliance with laws.

IT governance helps organisations use technology effectively while controlling these risks.

1.1 What is IT governance?

Key definition: IT Governance

IT governance refers to the predefined processes, structures and mechanisms that an organisation uses to ensure that IT resources are managed appropriately, efficiently and effectively in support of its business objectives.

In simple terms, IT governance determines how IT is managed, who is responsible for it, and how important IT decisions are made.

  • Ensure that technology supports business goals.
  • Make appropriate decisions about IT investments.
  • Identify and reduce potential risks.
  • Protect information and IT systems.
  • Use staff, hardware, software and budgets efficiently.
  • Meet legal and regulatory obligations.
  • Measure and improve IT performance.
📚 Example: IT governance in a school

A school uses a learning management system (LMS) for lessons, assignments and examination results. Governance defines who maintains the system, who can access student records, who approves software and server purchases, and what happens if the system stops working. The school's management might approve a new server, while an IT administrator manages accounts, security and backups.

1.2 Why is IT governance important?

Without effective IT governance With effective IT governance
IT investments may be wasted. IT expenditure is planned and justified.
Staff may be unsure of their duties. Roles and responsibilities are clear.
Security risks may be ignored. Risks are identified and managed.
IT may not support business goals. IT activities support organisational strategies.
Important decisions may be delayed. Decision-making authority is defined.
Legal obligations may be overlooked. Compliance is monitored.
💡 Important: IT governance is not just about securing computers. It also ensures that technology delivers real value to the organisation.

Section 02

Governance Structures and IT Policies

Effective governance needs clear, documented procedures that explain how employees should use IT systems and how important decisions are authorised.

2.1 Roles and responsibilities

Personnel Typical responsibility
Senior management Approves IT strategy, major expenditure and organisational policies.
IT manager Oversees IT services, planning, technical staff and resource allocation.
IT administrator Maintains systems, user accounts and operational IT services.
Security officer Oversees information security and related risks.
Employees Use technology responsibly and follow established policies.
Data protection officer, where appointed Oversees relevant data protection responsibilities.

These are illustrative roles; responsibilities depend on an organisation's size and structure.

2.2 Decision-making hierarchy

A decision-making hierarchy shows who has authority to make different decisions. Replacing a keyboard might be authorised by IT support, but replacing a central server could require senior management's approval.

IT AdministratorIdentifies the server problem and reports it.
↓
IT ManagerEvaluates the options, costs and risks.
↓
Senior ManagementMakes the final decision on the investment.
↓
IT DepartmentPlans and carries out the approved replacement.

2.3 What is an IT policy?

Key definition: IT Policy

An IT policy is a formal document containing rules, responsibilities, guidelines and procedures for the appropriate use and management of an organisation's IT resources.

Examples include business continuity, disaster recovery, risk management, acceptable use and information security policies.

2.4 IT governance vs IT policy

IT governance IT policy
Overall framework for directing and controlling IT. Specific rules and procedures for using and managing IT.
Determines who is responsible and authorised. Explains what users and staff should do.
Focuses on strategic goals, risk and performance. Focuses on required actions and behaviours.
Example: Management sets the approach to IT security. Example: Staff must use approved authentication methods.
Remember: Governance provides direction and accountability; policies translate that direction into practical rules and procedures.

Section 03

The Five Key Goals of IT Governance

🎯 1. Strategic alignment

IT activities and investments support overall business strategies and objectives.

🛡️ 2. Risk management

Identify threats and apply controls to reduce their likelihood or impact.

💻 3. Resource management

Plan the purchase, use and allocation of resources to minimise waste.

📈 4. Performance measurement

Use measures and indicators to evaluate IT effectiveness and improve performance.

⚖️ 5. Compliance and legal requirements

Meet relevant privacy, data protection, cybersecurity and other legal obligations.

3.1 Strategic alignment

Strategic alignment means that the organisation's IT strategy supports its business objectives. Technology investments should deliver useful results rather than being purchased without a clear purpose.

🛒 Example: Online retailer

A retailer wants to increase online sales. It could invest in a faster website, more secure payments and a customer relationship management (CRM) system. Each investment directly supports the business objective of improving sales and customer satisfaction.

Benefits: avoids unnecessary expenditure, improves return on investment and makes IT more useful to the organisation.

3.2 Risk management

Risk management identifies threats, estimates the consequences and introduces controls. Risks can include malware, hardware failure, unauthorised access, data loss, power failure and human error.

Risk Possible consequence Possible mitigation
Malware Damage to files or theft of information. Updates, security software and staff training.
Hardware failure Systems become unavailable. Maintenance, backups and spare equipment.
Unauthorised access Confidential information is exposed. Access restrictions and multifactor authentication.
Data loss Important records become unavailable. Regular, tested backups.
Power failure IT services are interrupted. Uninterruptible power supplies (UPS).
Human error Files are deleted or incorrectly changed. Training, permissions and backups.

3.3 Resource management

Organisations manage hardware, software, licences, data storage, network capacity, budgets and staff. The aim is to maximise value and minimise waste.

🏫 Example: School computer laboratory

A school wants 30 computers. It must consider purchase price, software requirements, maintenance costs and network capacity. Computers that are too expensive may waste money; computers that cannot run required applications may fail to meet the school's needs.

3.4 Performance measurement

Performance is measured through Key Performance Indicators (KPIs): measurable values used to assess progress towards objectives.

KPI What it measures
System uptime How often a service is available.
Average response time How quickly systems or support teams respond.
Number of security incidents Frequency of recorded security problems.
Recovery time How long it takes to restore disrupted services.
Spending against budget Whether IT costs stay within plans.
User satisfaction How users rate the IT service.
📊 Example: Website uptime

If a company sets a target of 99.9% availability for its ordering system, it can monitor actual uptime. Falling below the target prompts an investigation and improvement plan.

3.5 Compliance and legal requirements

Compliance means following applicable laws and requirements, including data protection, privacy, cybersecurity, confidentiality, intellectual property and software licensing rules.

🔐 Example: Student records

A school holds personal information including names, addresses and examination results. It should restrict access to authorised staff, protect stored records and establish appropriate data-breach response procedures.

Failing to comply can lead to legal penalties, financial losses, reputational damage and reduced customer trust. The exact obligations depend on location, activities and the information handled.

Section 04

Business Continuity Policy

4.1 What is business continuity?

Key definition: Business Continuity

The ability of an organisation to keep essential business activities operating during and after a serious disruption.

Key definition: Business Continuity Plan (BCP)

A documented set of procedures describing how an organisation will maintain or resume essential activities when unexpected incidents occur.

Disruptions can include cyberattacks, serious IT failures, electricity or internet outages, fires, floods, unavailable workplaces or the failure of an important service provider.

4.2 Why is it important?

  • Minimises disruption: alternative arrangements keep services operating.
  • Maintains confidence: customers can still receive important services.
  • Reduces financial losses: interruptions to transactions and productivity are limited.
  • Protects reputation: incidents can be managed professionally.
  • Clarifies responsibility: employees know what they must do.
  • Supports recovery: temporary operations continue while damaged systems are restored.

4.3 What should a BCP contain?

Component Purpose
Critical functions Identifies activities that must continue.
Roles and responsibilities States who makes emergency decisions and performs tasks.
Communication procedures Explains how staff, customers and relevant authorities are informed.
Fallback systems Identifies alternative tools and processes.
Alternative working arrangements Explains how staff can work if normal facilities are unavailable.
Incident investigation Establishes how the root cause is investigated.
Recovery coordination Explains how normal activities are resumed.
Review and improvement Keeps procedures updated after incidents and tests.

4.4 Example: Online learning system failure

🚨 IncidentThe school's learning management server stops working.
↓
1. AssessThe IT team checks the failure and informs school management.
↓
2. Activate alternativesTeachers distribute lessons through an approved alternative platform or printed resources.
↓
3. CommunicateStudents, teachers and parents receive information about temporary arrangements.
↓
✅ Continue teachingEssential educational activities continue while systems are repaired.
Key idea: A BCP is not simply a plan to repair technology. Its main focus is continuing essential activities while disruption is occurring.

4.5 Link with disaster recovery

Business continuity and disaster recovery are often linked or integrated. In the school example, the BCP provides alternative teaching arrangements, while the disaster recovery plan guides IT staff in restoring the learning platform.

Section 05

Disaster Recovery Policy

5.1 What is disaster recovery?

Key definition: Disaster Recovery

The process of restoring IT systems, data and services following a serious incident that causes damage, data loss or system unavailability.

Key definition: Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP)

A documented set of procedures describing how an organisation responds to an IT disaster and restores technology infrastructure, applications and data.

Possible disasters include failed servers, ransomware, accidental data deletion, fires, floods, natural disasters and major network failures.

5.2 Objectives of disaster recovery

  • Minimise downtime.
  • Restore important information and data.
  • Recover critical applications and services.
  • Reduce the financial impact of failures.
  • Allocate recovery responsibilities clearly.
  • Establish procedures for different disaster scenarios.
  • Test and update recovery arrangements.

5.3 Elements of a DRP

Element What it includes
Recovery objectives Targets for service restoration time and acceptable data loss.
Roles and responsibilities Staff responsible for different recovery tasks.
Backup strategies How backups are created, protected, stored and restored.
Recovery procedures Steps to bring affected systems back into operation.
Contingency plans Alternative arrangements if recovery is delayed.
Testing Checks that backups and restoration procedures work.
Review and updates Changes required as technology and risks evolve.

5.4 Data backup and recovery

A backup is a separate copy of data that can be used to restore information if the original becomes lost, damaged or unavailable.

Backup type Description Example
Full Copies all selected data. Entire database backed up on Sunday.
Incremental Copies changes since the most recent backup of any type. Monday's backup contains changes since Sunday's backup.
Differential Copies changes since the last full backup. Wednesday's backup contains all changes since Sunday's full backup.

Backups should be secure and tested. A backup that cannot be restored does not provide reliable protection.

5.5 Recovery targets: RTO and RPO

⏱️ Recovery Time Objective (RTO)

Target maximum time allowed to restore a service after disruption. An RTO of two hours means the organisation aims to restore service within two hours.

🗂️ Recovery Point Objective (RPO)

Target maximum amount of data loss measured in time. An RPO of 30 minutes means the organisation aims to avoid losing more than 30 minutes of data.

5.6 Example: Database server failure at a bank

  1. Detect the database server failure.
  2. Assess the systems and data affected.
  3. Activate the disaster recovery team.
  4. Switch to a suitable backup system, if available.
  5. Restore information from a verified backup.
  6. Check the accuracy of account balances and transactions.
  7. Return services to normal.
  8. Investigate the cause and improve recovery procedures.

Effective disaster recovery limits downtime, financial losses and damage to trust.

Section 06

Business Continuity vs Disaster Recovery

The two concepts are related but have different main purposes.

Feature Business continuity Disaster recovery
Main purpose Keep essential activities operating. Restore affected IT systems and data.
Main focus Organisation-wide operations. IT infrastructure and information.
Typical response Use alternative ways of working and fallback systems. Recover servers, applications, networks and backups.
Common responsibility Business management and departments. IT recovery teams with management support.
School example Teachers use an alternative lesson platform. Technicians restore the primary learning platform.

🏢 Business continuity

“How do we keep working during the problem?”

💾 Disaster recovery

“How do we restore the affected technology?”

Remember: Business continuity is about continuing business operations. Disaster recovery is about restoring IT systems and data. Organisations often need both.

Section 07

Risk Management Policy

7.1 What is risk management?

Key definition: Risk Management

The systematic process of identifying, assessing, reducing and monitoring potential threats that could negatively affect an organisation or its IT systems.

A risk is the possibility of an event occurring and causing an undesirable outcome. Risks cannot always be eliminated; organisations therefore try to reduce their likelihood or the harm they may cause.

7.2 Purpose of the policy

  • Identify and document potential risks.
  • Assess the probability and severity of each risk.
  • Decide which risks require attention first.
  • Introduce preventive and protective controls.
  • Assign responsibility for managing risks.
  • Monitor risks and review the effectiveness of the controls.

7.3 Risk management process

1. IdentifyList threats such as data loss, malware and system failure.
↓
2. AssessEstimate likelihood and severity.
↓
3. PrioritiseDecide which risks need urgent attention.
↓
4. MitigateImplement security measures, backups and training.
↓
5. Monitor and review ↻Check that controls work and update the assessment regularly.

7.4 Risk assessment

Two common factors are probability (how likely the incident is) and severity (how serious the consequences would be). A simple semi-quantitative approach calculates a risk score by multiplying the two ratings.

Risk Score = Probability × Severity
Rating Probability Severity
1 Very unlikely Negligible
2 Unlikely Minor
3 Possible Moderate
4 Likely Major
5 Very likely Critical

Worked example: A server failure has a probability rating of 3 and a severity rating of 5. The risk score is 3 × 5 = 15. In this example, a larger score represents higher priority.

7.5 Methods of risk mitigation

Risk Possible mitigation
Ransomware Maintain protected backups, update systems and train staff.
Unauthorised access Use permissions and multifactor authentication.
Data loss Make and test regular backups.
Hardware failure Use preventive maintenance and suitable redundant systems.
Network outage Monitor the network and consider alternative connectivity.
Human error Offer training and establish clear procedures.
Physical damage Protect premises and equipment.

7.6 How risk policies work with other policies

🔒 Example: Ransomware attack
  • Risk management: identifies and evaluates the ransomware threat.
  • Security policy: defines preventive measures such as updates, permissions and malware protection.
  • Disaster recovery: explains how systems and data will be restored.
  • Business continuity: explains how essential activities can continue during restoration.

These policies should be linked rather than written and applied in isolation.

Section 08

User Policy (Acceptable Use Policy – AUP)

8.1 What is an AUP?

Key definition: Acceptable Use Policy (AUP)

A formal document that defines acceptable and unacceptable behaviour when users access an organisation's IT resources, including computers, networks, applications and online services.

An AUP explains what employees and other users can and cannot do when using organisational technology.

8.2 Why is an AUP necessary?

  1. Prevents misuse of IT resources.
  2. Reduces security risks caused by unsafe behaviour.
  3. Protects confidential and sensitive information.
  4. Encourages responsible internet and email usage.
  5. Establishes professional conduct standards.
  6. Explains the consequences of policy violations.

8.3 Common areas covered by an AUP

💻 Computers and software

Rules for using devices, installing applications and modifying settings.

🌐 Internet usage

Rules for appropriate browsing and restrictions on harmful or inappropriate sites.

✉️ Email communication

Professional conduct, suspicious attachments, phishing and message handling.

🔐 Sensitive information

Rules for authorised access, storage and sharing of confidential data.

🤝 Professional behaviour

Expected conduct when interacting with colleagues, customers and external stakeholders.

8.4 Acceptable vs unacceptable behaviour

Acceptable Unacceptable
Use approved software for work. Install unauthorised applications.
Keep passwords confidential. Share passwords with colleagues.
Report suspicious emails. Open suspicious attachments without checks.
Access only authorised records. View confidential information without permission.
Communicate professionally. Send abusive or inappropriate messages.
Follow data-handling procedures. Share sensitive information with unauthorised people.

8.5 Consequences of breaking an AUP

Depending on the breach and organisational procedures, consequences may include warnings, additional training, restrictions on IT access, disciplinary measures, dismissal in serious cases or legal action where applicable.

8.6 Example: AUP in a school

🏫 School AUP

Students must use school computers for approved educational purposes, keep passwords private, respect others online, avoid unauthorised software, not access another student's account and report suspicious messages. Repeated violations may result in sanctions according to the school's rules.

Important: An AUP supports system security and sets professional expectations for how users behave and communicate.

Section 09

Comparing the Four Important IT Policies

Policy Question it answers Main purpose
Business continuity How can essential activities continue during disruption? Maintain operations.
Disaster recovery How can damaged systems and data be restored? Recover technology and data.
Risk management What could go wrong and how can we reduce the risk? Identify, assess and mitigate threats.
Acceptable use What are users allowed and forbidden to do? Promote secure, responsible IT usage.

Although these policies have different purposes, they should be designed to work together as part of the organisation's governance framework.

Section 10

Applying IT Governance in a Real Organisation

10.1 Case study: Online education platform

Consider an online education platform used by 500 students and 20 teachers. It provides lessons, assignments, examinations and progress reports.

Governance area Example application
Strategic alignment Invest in features that improve teaching and learning.
Risk management Assess data loss, cyberattacks and server failure.
Resource management Budget for hosting, storage and technical support.
Performance measurement Monitor uptime, speed and technical incidents.
Compliance Protect student data according to applicable requirements.
Business continuity Arrange alternative access to lessons during an outage.
Disaster recovery Restore the platform and student records from backups.
Acceptable use Set account security and online conduct rules for staff and learners.

10.2 Scenario: Serious cyberattack

  1. Risk management helps identify the threat and plan controls.
  2. Security procedures help contain the attack.
  3. Business continuity provides approved alternatives for lessons.
  4. Disaster recovery guides system and data restoration.
  5. Acceptable use defines security and reporting duties for users.
  6. IT governance coordinates responsibilities, resources and decisions.

This shows why organisations need a coordinated governance framework, not unrelated policies.

Section 11

Practical Activity: IT Risk Assessment

The original Topic 14.7 lesson concludes with an activity asking students to investigate common risk assessment methodologies and assess IT risks in a school or college.

The suggested assessment table includes: potential risk, probability, severity, risk score, and comments/potential mitigation.

11.1 Example completed school assessment

Potential risk Probability (1–5) Severity (1–5) Risk score Comments / mitigation
Malware infection 4 4 16 Security software, updates and awareness training.
Server failure 3 5 15 Maintenance, reliable equipment and tested backups.
Power failure 3 3 9 Use UPS devices for critical systems.
Unauthorised access 3 5 15 Use authentication and access controls.
Accidental data deletion 3 4 12 Backups, permissions and training.
Network failure 4 3 12 Monitor the network and consider alternative connectivity.

Illustrative values only: a real assessment should justify its ratings using evidence about the school's actual systems.

11.2 Common risk assessment methodologies

Qualitative

Describes risk using categories such as low, medium and high. Example: server failure is medium likelihood and high impact.

Semi-quantitative

Uses numerical rating scales, such as 1–5, and calculates a score. Example: 3 × 5 = 15.

Quantitative

Uses numerical estimates such as incident probability and potential financial loss. Example: estimating the cost of an extended website outage.

11.3 Suggested steps

  1. Identify school IT systems such as computers, servers, databases and networks.
  2. List the risks affecting each system.
  3. Estimate each risk's probability.
  4. Estimate the severity of its consequences.
  5. Calculate a score using the chosen method.
  6. Suggest realistic mitigation strategies.
  7. Review high-priority risks and recommend improvements.
