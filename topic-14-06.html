Expert Systems

Understand how knowledge-based systems imitate specialist decision-making, how a knowledge base and inference engine work together, and the benefits and limitations of expert-system chatbots in organisations.

📚 Using IT Systems in Organisations📝 Detailed Lesson Notes
Lesson overview: An expert system uses stored expertise and rules to provide advice or solve problems within a defined field. This lesson covers its components, reasoning techniques, real-world uses, strengths, weaknesses and the textbook's customer-support chatbot case study.
🧠
Section 01

Introduction to Expert Systems

1.1 What is an expert system?

An expert system is a computer-based information system designed to imitate the decision-making ability of a human expert in a particular field. It uses stored facts, rules and logical reasoning to analyse problems, draw conclusions and recommend possible solutions.

Key definition

An expert system is a knowledge-based computer system that uses a collection of facts and rules, together with an inference engine, to solve problems or provide expert advice within a specific knowledge domain.

1.2 Examples of expert systems

🏥 Medical support

Analyses symptoms, medical history and test results to suggest conditions for healthcare professionals to investigate.

💻 Computer troubleshooting

Asks questions about technical faults and suggests steps such as checking settings, cables or software configuration.

🚗 Vehicle diagnosis

Uses symptoms and diagnostic fault codes to identify possible problems with a vehicle and recommend inspections.

🏦 Financial services

Applies predefined criteria to support loan, insurance and risk-assessment decisions.

1.3 Why are expert systems useful?

Human experts have valuable specialist knowledge but may be expensive, busy or unavailable outside working hours. An expert system can make some of that knowledge available immediately.

  • Make routine decisions more quickly.
  • Provide more consistent recommendations.
  • Reduce dependence on human experts for common problems.
  • Make specialist knowledge accessible to many users.
  • Reduce some operating costs and support employee decision-making.
Remember

Expert systems normally support decision-making rather than completely replace skilled humans, especially for unusual or high-risk problems.

Section 02

Characteristics of Expert Systems

2.1 Knowledge-based

Expert systems depend on facts, rules and practical knowledge supplied by human specialists. This information is organised and stored so that the system can apply it to a problem.

Example – Network troubleshooting

An experienced technician knows that a computer connected to Wi-Fi but unable to access the internet may have incorrect IP settings. That knowledge can be written as a diagnostic rule.

IF Wi-Fi is connected AND other devices can use the internet AND this computer has invalid IP settings THEN recommend checking its IP configuration.

2.2 Narrow scope (knowledge domain)

An expert system is usually designed for a specific, limited area of expertise called its knowledge domain. A system for diagnosing printer problems cannot automatically diagnose car-engine faults because it does not have the relevant rules and facts.

2.3 Static knowledge

Traditional rule-based expert systems do not automatically learn or improve as new data appears. If a software update changes how a setting works, a human expert or knowledge engineer may need to update the knowledge base.

Important distinction

Traditional expert systems are usually static, but some modern hybrid systems combine rules with machine learning.

2.4 Rule-based reasoning

Expert systems often follow IF–THEN rules. When the stated condition is satisfied, the system can draw a conclusion or suggest an action.

Basic decision rule
IFCondition is true
THENConclusion or action
Printer example

IF the printer is switched on AND it has paper AND a paper-jam warning is displayed, THEN recommend safely checking the paper path for an obstruction.

Section 03

Main Components of an Expert System

The two main components are the knowledge base and inference engine. A practical system usually also has a user interface; it may include working memory and an explanation facility.

Basic expert system structure
👤 UserEnters the problem and answers questions
↕
💬 User interfaceCollects input and displays advice
↕
⚙️ Inference engineApplies rules and reaches conclusions
↙   ↘
📚 Knowledge baseFacts, rules and expert knowledge
🗂️ Working memoryFacts about the current case

3.1 Knowledge base

The knowledge base is a repository of specialist information. It contains the knowledge required for the expert system to make decisions in its particular domain.

Type of knowledge Explanation Example
Facts Information known about a subject or situation. A printer needs paper to print.
Rules Logical statements that link conditions to conclusions or actions. IF there is no paper, THEN load paper.
Heuristics Practical strategies based on experts' experience. Checking cable connections is often a useful early troubleshooting step.

Example – Laptop troubleshooting knowledge base

Rule Condition or symptom Possible recommendation
1 Battery is empty and laptop will not turn on. Connect the charger.
2 Laptop is running but display appears dark. Check brightness and display settings.
3 Laptop overheats. Check vents and cooling.
4 Laptop cannot connect to Wi-Fi. Check wireless settings and network connection.
5 Laptop operates very slowly. Check running applications and available resources.

A useful knowledge base must be accurate, sufficiently complete, consistent and up to date, not merely large.

3.2 Inference engine

The inference engine is the decision-making component. It examines the facts about a current problem, looks for applicable rules in the knowledge base, and uses logical reasoning to arrive at a conclusion.

📚 Knowledge base

Stores what the system knows.

⚙️ Inference engine

Determines what conclusions follow from that knowledge.

Printer diagnosis example

  1. Check powerCustomer confirms the printer is switched on.
  2. Check paperCustomer confirms paper is available.
  3. Ask about errorsCustomer reports a paper-jam warning.
  4. Apply the matching ruleThe inference engine selects the relevant troubleshooting rule.
  5. Suggest an actionThe system explains how to safely inspect the paper path.

3.3 User interface

The user interface allows users to enter symptoms, answer questions, view recommendations and request additional assistance. It might be a chatbot, website, mobile application or troubleshooting wizard.

3.4 Other supporting components

🗂️ Working memory

Temporarily holds information about the current problem, such as the answers a customer provides.

💡 Explanation facility

Shows why a recommendation was made and which facts or rules supported it.

🛠️ Knowledge acquisition

Supports the collection, organisation and updating of expert knowledge.

Section 04

How an Expert System Works

The system asks questions to collect information, applies the rules that fit the known facts and proposes a suitable conclusion or action.

Expert system decision-making process
1. User describes the problem“My laptop cannot connect to Wi-Fi.”
↓
2. System gathers informationAsks about connections, settings and symptoms.
↓
3. Inference engine applies rulesFinds applicable rules in the knowledge base.
↓
4. System draws a conclusionIdentifies a possible cause.
↓
5. System recommends a solutionProvides troubleshooting steps.

4.1 Example – Internet connection problem

A customer reports that their laptop cannot connect to the internet. The system asks diagnostic questions.

System question Customer answer
Is Wi-Fi enabled? Yes
Is the laptop connected to a Wi-Fi network? Yes
Can other devices access the internet? Yes
Does the laptop have a valid IP configuration? No
IF Wi-Fi is enabled AND the laptop is connected to Wi-Fi AND other devices can access the internet AND the laptop has invalid IP settings THEN possible cause = incorrect IP configuration recommend checking IP settings and using the operating system's network troubleshooter.

The inference engine matches the known facts to this rule and presents its recommendation. If the problem continues, the system should investigate other possible causes or refer the case to a technician.

Important

An expert system's recommendation is not guaranteed to be correct. Its accuracy depends on the quality of the user's information and the knowledge base.

Section 05

Reasoning Methods Used by Expert Systems

5.1 Forward chaining

Forward chaining is a data-driven reasoning method. The system begins with known facts, applies relevant rules and works towards possible conclusions.

Forward chaining – facts to conclusion
Known factPrinter displays a paper-jam warning.
↓
Apply matching ruleIF a paper-jam warning appears, investigate the paper path.
↓
ConclusionRecommend checking for jammed paper.

5.2 Backward chaining

Backward chaining is a goal-driven reasoning method. The system begins with a possible conclusion and checks whether the necessary supporting facts are true.

Example – Laptop battery

The system considers the hypothesis “The battery is discharged.” It checks whether the laptop fails to turn on, is disconnected from external power and has an empty battery. If the necessary conditions are met, the system supports this diagnosis and recommends charging the laptop.

Forward chaining Backward chaining
Starts with known facts. Starts with a possible conclusion or goal.
Applies rules to discover conclusions. Checks which facts support a conclusion.
Data-driven reasoning. Goal-driven reasoning.
Useful for exploring possible diagnoses. Useful for checking a specific hypothesis.

Section 06

Expert Systems, AI and Machine Learning

Expert systems are a traditional form of artificial intelligence (AI). They are often called symbolic AI because they represent knowledge through explicit facts and rules. Machine learning is another AI approach that learns patterns from training data.

Feature Traditional expert system Machine learning system
Knowledge source Facts and rules supplied by experts. Patterns learned from training data.
Decision-making Applies explicit logical rules. Uses a trained predictive model.
Automatic learning Usually does not learn automatically. Can improve through training or retraining.
Updating Rules are generally changed manually. Models can be retrained with new data.
Explainability Rule paths can often be traced. Some models are difficult to explain.
Requirements Needs specialist knowledge. Often needs suitable training data.
Scope Usually focused on a narrow domain. Depends on the model and training.

6.1 Example – Suspicious banking transactions

📏 Expert-system approach

A predefined rule flags transactions that exceed a specified amount and originate from a high-risk location. Employees change the rule when the bank updates its criteria.

📊 Machine-learning approach

A model learns patterns from historic transactions labelled as legitimate or fraudulent, then estimates the risk of new transactions.

Hybrid systems

Some modern applications combine human-written rules with machine-learning models. This does not change the basic distinction between rule-based reasoning and pattern learning.

Section 07

Advantages of Expert Systems

Advantage Detailed explanation
24/7 availability Can offer advice at any time, even when human experts are unavailable.
Fast decisions Applies stored rules quickly to provide answers for common problems.
Consistency Normally produces the same advice for the same inputs and rules.
Reduced operating costs Automates routine tasks and reduces staff time spent on repetitive enquiries.
Preserved knowledge Keeps valuable specialist knowledge available when employees leave.
Improved accessibility Lets more users access specialist advice without an in-person appointment.
Scalability Can support many users if the organisation has enough computing resources.
Fewer routine human mistakes Consistently follows stored rules without tiredness or distraction.
Example – Online technical support

A software company receives hundreds of daily requests about password resets, incorrect settings and installations. An expert system can handle many routine cases so that human technicians spend more time on complex problems.

Section 08

Limitations and Disadvantages

🎯 8.1 Limited knowledge

The system can only use knowledge stored in its domain. A software-installation chatbot may not understand hardware faults.

🔁 8.2 No automatic learning

Traditional expert systems need manual updates when problems, procedures or technologies change.

💰 8.3 High development costs

Specialists, knowledge engineers, programmers, testing and maintenance may all be expensive.

❓ 8.4 Unusual problems

Unfamiliar, complex, uncertain or conflicting situations can be difficult to represent with rules.

📚 8.5 Knowledge-base dependence

Outdated or incorrect rules can lead to inaccurate recommendations.

👩‍🔧 8.6 Limited human judgement

A system may lack the contextual understanding and practical flexibility of a skilled human expert.

8.7 Security and privacy concerns

Expert systems may process sensitive customer, financial or medical information. Organisations must use appropriate security measures and protect personal data against unauthorised access or misuse.

Example – Outdated instructions

If a new software version removes a setting, an old troubleshooting rule could give customers useless or misleading instructions.

Section 09

Developing and Maintaining an Expert System

  1. 9.1 Identify the knowledge domainDefine exactly which problems the system will address, such as login, installation and configuration issues.
  2. 9.2 Acquire expert knowledgeGather reliable facts and troubleshooting procedures from specialists, manuals and approved documents. This is called knowledge acquisition.
  3. 9.3 Build the knowledge baseOrganise knowledge into facts, rules and heuristics. Check that the rules are accurate and consistent.
  4. 9.4 Develop the inference engine and interfaceImplement reasoning procedures and give users a clear way to enter symptoms and view recommendations.
  5. 9.5 Test and validateTest real cases, incorrect inputs, missing facts, conflicting rules, recommendations and the process for referring cases to people.
  6. 9.6 Maintain and updateReview expert knowledge and update rules whenever software, products, procedures or regulations change.
Focus on knowledge acquisition

Experienced employees often use practical judgement that is difficult to turn into simple rules. Extracting and validating that knowledge is a major development challenge.

Section 11

Important Considerations in Organisations

11.1 Reliability

Expert-system recommendations should be accurate and dependable. Organisations need suitable testing, monitoring and maintenance because incorrect conclusions may waste time or cause harm.

11.2 Human supervision

There must be a clear way to refer unusual, high-risk or unresolved cases to a qualified human. In healthcare or safety-critical environments, expert advice should not replace appropriate professional judgement.

11.3 Data quality

If a user enters incomplete or incorrect details, the inference engine may apply the wrong rule. This illustrates Garbage In, Garbage Out (GIGO): poor input can lead to poor output.

GIGO example

If a customer incorrectly says that a router is functioning normally, the system may rule out a router fault and suggest less relevant solutions.

11.4 Explainability

A rule-based expert system can often explain which facts and rules produced a recommendation. This helps human experts check decisions and gives users more confidence.

Explanation example

“Check the IP configuration because your laptop is connected to Wi-Fi, other devices can use the internet, and this laptop has invalid IP settings.”

Section 12

Summary of Key Concepts

Key term Meaning
Expert system Computer system that imitates specialist decision-making in a particular domain.
Knowledge domain The specific subject area covered by an expert system.
Knowledge base Repository of facts, rules and expert knowledge.
Inference engine Component that applies rules to reach conclusions.
Facts Stored statements about a subject or current situation.
Rules Logical conditions linking facts to conclusions or actions.
Heuristics Practical strategies drawn from expert experience.
User interface How a user interacts with the system.
Forward chaining Reasoning from known facts towards conclusions.
Backward chaining Testing whether known facts support a possible conclusion.
Knowledge acquisition Collecting and organising expert knowledge.
Static knowledge Knowledge that does not automatically update itself.
Explanation facility Explains how a conclusion was reached.
Working memory Temporary facts relating to the current case.
Most important distinction

The knowledge base stores what the system knows. The inference engine uses that knowledge to reason about the user's problem and recommend possible solutions.

Expert systems can improve the speed, consistency and accessibility of decisions, but their success depends on the accuracy of their rules, good-quality input, regular updating and the availability of human experts for difficult cases.
