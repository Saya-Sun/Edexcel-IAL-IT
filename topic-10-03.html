Section 01

Introduction to Effective Web Design

A website should not only look attractive. It should also be easy to understand, simple to navigate and suitable for its intended audience.

Key definition · Design principlesDesign principles are guidelines that help web designers organise content, select colours and arrange visual elements to create effective, user-friendly websites.

When a student opens an online learning website, they should be able to identify the lesson title, find the navigation menu and locate the content they need without confusion.

Section 02

Visual Hierarchy

2.1 What is visual hierarchy?

Key definition · Visual hierarchyVisual hierarchy is the arrangement of elements on a web page according to their importance, so users notice the most important information first.

Users do not examine every element at once. Their attention is drawn to elements because of their size, colour, position or appearance. Designers use these features to direct attention.

Example: On a shopping website, the designer may want users to notice the product name first, followed by the product image, price and purchase button.

2.3 Five techniques used to create visual hierarchy

Technique Explanation Example
Size Larger elements generally attract attention. A main heading is larger than paragraph text.
Colour Bright or contrasting colours make elements stand out. A purple action button on a white page.
Alignment Positioning and alignment influence what attracts attention. Important information in a prominent position.
Proximity Related elements are placed close together to show a connection. A product price near its image.
White space Empty areas separate elements and draw attention to important content. Extra space around a main heading.

2.4 Understanding white space

Key definition · White spaceWhite space, or negative space, is the empty area between elements on a page. It does not have to be white; it may be any background colour.

White space improves readability, separates sections, reduces visual clutter and emphasises important information. Paragraphs without spacing are difficult to read, while clear gaps between headings, paragraphs and images help organise the page.

2.5 Advantages of visual hierarchy

Find information quickly

Important headings and actions are immediately noticeable.

Understand page structure

Users can see how sections relate to each other.

Reduce confusion

Clear differences in importance make the layout easier to follow.

Support user actions

Buttons and relevant content are easier to locate.

Educational website example: The lesson title should be more prominent than its description. Section headings should be distinguishable from body text, while navigation remains easy to identify.

Section 03

Flow

3.1 What is flow in web design?

Key definition · FlowFlow is the path or sequence of actions that a designer intends users to follow when navigating a website or completing a task.

A well-designed website guides users naturally from one stage to the next. Users should understand what to do without needing further explanation.

3.2 Example: Online shopping flow

1Find a productSearch or browse the catalogue
2Choose optionsSelect the size and colour
3Add to basketReview selected items
4CheckoutEnter delivery and payment details
5ConfirmationDisplay the order result
Why order matters: Users should select a sweater's size before placing it in the basket. Asking for the size after checkout has started interrupts the process.

3.3 Why is flow important?

Logical flow helps people complete tasks efficiently and with fewer errors. Poor flow may force users to repeat actions, look for missing controls or move backwards through several pages.

3.4 Features of good flow

Feature Explanation
Logical order Tasks are arranged in a sensible sequence.
Clear navigation Users can identify where to go next.
Consistency Similar tasks use familiar controls and layouts.
Feedback The website confirms whether an action succeeded or failed.
Minimal unnecessary steps Users are not required to complete irrelevant actions.

Section 04

Colour Theory

4.1 What is colour theory?

Key definition · Colour theoryColour theory studies how colours relate to one another, how they can be combined and how they influence the appearance and perception of a design.

Designers use colour theory to create appealing combinations, develop a consistent identity, draw attention to important elements and improve readability. For example, using purple and white throughout an educational site provides a consistent appearance.

4.2 Understanding the colour wheel

A colour wheel is a circular arrangement of colours that illustrates their relationships. It is commonly used to choose complementary, analogous and triadic colour schemes.

How designers use it

Colours close together on a wheel usually produce a harmonious scheme. Colours far apart can create stronger contrast.

The following three relationships are especially important for this topic.

4.3–4.5 Three colour schemes

Complementary

Two colours opposite each other on the traditional wheel, such as purple and yellow. Strong contrast.

Analogous

Neighbouring colours, such as blue, blue-violet and violet. A harmonious effect.

Triadic

Three approximately equally spaced colours, such as red, yellow and blue. Colourful variety.

Complementary

Useful for emphasising a button or highlight. Too much of both strong colours may feel overwhelming.

Analogous

Useful for related backgrounds, headings and decorative elements that work well together.

Triadic

Often works best with one dominant colour and the other two used as accents.

4.6 Comparing the three colour schemes

Scheme Relationship Typical effect
Complementary Opposite on the colour wheel Strong contrast and emphasis
Analogous Adjacent on the colour wheel Harmony and consistency
Triadic Evenly spaced around the wheel Colourful, balanced variety

4.7 Colours and human perception

Colours may influence how people feel about a website. However, the associations differ between individuals, cultures and contexts.

Colour Possible association Example use
Red Danger, urgency or good fortune Warning messages or promotional highlights
Yellow Happiness and optimism Decorative highlights or cheerful designs
Green Calmness and reassurance Success messages or nature-related sites
Important: Colour associations are not universal. Red may suggest danger in one setting but good fortune in another. Designers should consider the intended audience rather than assuming everyone responds identically.

Section 05

Balance

5.1 What is balance?

Key definition · BalanceBalance is the arrangement of visual elements so that a web page looks stable and well organised, without one area appearing unnecessarily crowded or dominant.

5.2 Symmetrical and 5.3 Asymmetrical balance

Symmetrical balance
Similar content appears on either side of a centre line. The result is orderly and predictable.
Asymmetrical balance
Different-sized elements can still work together to create a balanced, dynamic layout.

Symmetrical balance suits more formal, structured layouts. Asymmetrical balance is useful for modern layouts where elements do not need to be identical.

5.4 Visual weight

Key definition · Visual weightVisual weight describes how strongly an element attracts attention.

A large image, bright colour or bold heading may have more visual weight than a small paragraph. Designers distribute visual weight to keep the page balanced.

Example: A large image on the left of a page can be balanced by a prominent heading and several smaller text elements on the right.

Section 06

Contrast

6.1 What is contrast?

Key definition · ContrastContrast is the noticeable difference between visual elements. Designers can create it through colour, size, shape, font weight or spacing.

Contrast helps people distinguish elements and recognise important information. Dark text on white generally reads more easily than light-grey text on a nearly identical light-grey background.

6.2 Types of contrast

Type Explanation Example
Colour Difference between colours Dark text on a light background
Size Difference in element size A large main heading and smaller paragraphs
Weight Difference in text thickness Bold headings and regular body text
Shape Difference in visual shape A rounded button among rectangular blocks
Spacing Difference in surrounding space A call-to-action set apart from other content

6.3 Text and background contrast

✕ Low contrastThis text is difficult to read.
✓ High contrastThis text is much easier to read.

6.4 Brightness and contrast ratios

The contrast ratio measures the difference in relative luminance between two colours. A higher contrast ratio generally means a larger light–dark difference.

Accessibility clarification · WCAG 2.2 Level AA

For applicable text, the minimum contrast ratios are:

Text type Minimum contrast ratio
Ordinary text 4.5:1
Large text 3:1

These requirements have defined exceptions. The source notes clarify that the textbook lists 4.5:1 for both categories.

Practical example: Before using text on a coloured button, check that the text and button background have enough contrast to be readable.

Section 07

Balance vs Contrast

Balance and contrast work together, but serve different purposes.

Balance Contrast
Creates visual stability Creates visual differences
Distributes visual weight Emphasises important elements
Produces a harmonious layout Helps users distinguish elements
Can be symmetrical or asymmetrical Can involve colour, size, weight or shape
Balance = organisation

Two well-spaced lesson cards in a row can create a stable layout.

Contrast = emphasis

A prominent purple heading and smaller dark text help signal what matters.

An effective design uses balance to organise the page and contrast to guide attention.

Section 09

Common Misunderstandings

✕“Visual hierarchy means making everything large.”
No. If all elements are equally prominent, users cannot easily identify what matters most.
✕“Flow only refers to navigation menus.”
No. It includes the entire sequence of user actions needed to complete a goal.
✕“Complementary and analogous schemes are the same.”
No. Complementary colours are opposite on the wheel, while analogous colours are adjacent.
✕“Balanced layouts must be symmetrical.”
No. Different-sized elements can create asymmetrical balance.
✕“Attractive colours always make text readable.”
No. Readability also depends on sufficient contrast between text and background.
✅
Section 10

Conclusion

Effective web design depends on several connected principles, not just attractive colours or decorative elements.

Visual hierarchy

Helps users notice important content first.

Flow

Guides users through a logical sequence of actions.

Colour theory

Helps create suitable, consistent and harmonious colour combinations.

Balance and contrast

Organise the layout, highlight information and improve readability.

When these principles are used together, a website communicates information clearly, supports user interaction and provides a better overall user experience.
