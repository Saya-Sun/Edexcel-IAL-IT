1. Introduction to Transaction Processing

1.1 What is transaction processing?

KEY DEFINITION · Transaction Processing (TP)

Transaction processing is the process of managing individual tasks that involve changes to data within an information system.

A transaction is an action that reads, adds, updates or deletes data as part of a particular operation. Transaction processing is commonly used wherever an organisation records purchases, payments, orders or inventory movements.

🏦

Financial transactions

Bank transfers, card payments and electronic bill payments.

🛒

Sales transactions

Purchasing products at a store checkout or through an online shop.

📦

Inventory transactions

Updating stock quantities after sales, deliveries or returns.

🎟️

Order transactions

Recording online orders, bookings and reservations.

The main purpose of transaction processing is to maintain data integrity so that stored data remains accurate, complete and consistent. Transactions are typically short-lived and self-contained, but often interact with large databases.

KEY DEFINITION · Data Integrity

Data integrity refers to keeping stored information accurate, complete and consistent throughout its use and processing.

1.2 How transaction processing works

Consider an online shop selling laptops. When a customer makes a purchase, several related operations take place:

🛒

1. Customer places an order

The customer chooses a laptop and confirms the purchase.

↓
💳

2. Payment is processed

The system requests authorisation for the payment.

↓

The available quantity is reduced to reflect the purchase.

↓

4. Order is recorded

The order details are saved and the customer receives confirmation.

Important: The operations must be coordinated correctly. If a customer is charged but the order is not recorded, the system has not completed the purchase correctly. Real payment processing may involve separate payment and database systems, so additional coordination is required.

1.3 Why transaction processing is important

Importance Explanation
Data accuracy Ensures that transaction records contain correct information.
Data integrity Prevents incomplete or conflicting changes to stored data.
Efficiency Automates routine operations and reduces manual work.
Reliability Helps systems handle transactions without losing important information.
Customer confidence Allows users to trust purchases, payments and reservations.
Up-to-date records Provides current stock, order and account information.
💡 Example · Supermarket stock

A supermarket has 50 bottles of water. A customer buys 6 bottles, so the stock database should show 44 bottles. Without this update, employees might make decisions using incorrect stock information.

1.4 Characteristics of transaction processing

⏱️

Short-lived

A transaction usually takes a short time, for example recording a single purchase.

🎯

Self-contained

Each transaction represents a particular operation, such as reserving a seat.

🗄️

Database interaction

Transactions read, insert or change records in a database.

✅

Consistent and reliable

Systems aim to avoid incorrect and incomplete updates.

📈

High volumes

Businesses may need to process many transactions at the same time.

🔗

Part of larger systems

Small transactions often interact with a wider IT system, such as inventory and finance databases.

Key point: Transaction processing is not just recording a purchase. It keeps important business information correct while actions take place.

2. Electronic Point of Sale (EPOS)

2.1 What is an EPOS system?

KEY DEFINITION · Electronic Point of Sale

An EPOS system is a computer-based system used to process sales transactions and manage associated business information.

Modern stores, supermarkets, restaurants and cafés often use EPOS systems rather than traditional cash registers. Unlike a basic cash register, an EPOS system can link sales activity with inventory databases, customer records and back-office processes.

🏪

Retail shops

Scan products, record purchases and calculate totals.

🍽️

Restaurants and cafés

Take orders, process bills and track popular items.

🛒

Supermarkets

Process high volumes of transactions and update stock automatically.

📱

Self-service checkouts

Allow customers to scan and pay for items themselves.

2.2 Components of an EPOS system

An EPOS setup may use some or all of the following components:

Component Function
Touchscreen / computer Allows employees to enter orders and operate the system.
Barcode scanner Reads product barcodes to identify goods quickly.
Card / contactless reader Accepts supported cards and smartphone payments.
Cash drawer Stores cash received from customers.
Receipt printer Prints details of completed sales.
EPOS software Calculates totals, records transactions and manages sales data.
Stock database Stores product information and available quantities.

A small café may use a tablet-based system, while a large supermarket may use dedicated checkout terminals.

2.3 Main functions of EPOS systems

A. Sales processing

Sales processing involves recording products or services purchased and handling customer payments.

  • Identify products from barcodes or product selections.
  • Retrieve product prices and calculate totals.
  • Process supported payment methods.
  • Record completed sales and produce receipts.
💡 Example · Shop checkout

A customer buys items costing $10, $15 and $20. The EPOS system calculates the total of $45 and records the transaction after payment.

Benefit: Faster and more accurate checkout processing.

B. Inventory management

An EPOS system can connect to a stock database and adjust product quantities after a sale. When stock reaches a predefined reorder level, the system may automatically initiate stock replenishment.

  • Maintains up-to-date stock information.
  • Reduces manual record keeping.
  • Helps avoid running out of popular products.
  • Supports more efficient stock replenishment.
  • Helps reduce unnecessary orders.

C. Customer insights

EPOS systems collect detailed sales data, helping managers identify popular and unpopular products, purchasing trends, busy times and changes in customer demand.

💡 Example · Café demand

A café finds that iced coffee sells most frequently between 1 p.m. and 4 p.m. It can schedule staff and prepare enough ingredients during that period.

Benefit: More informed decisions about stock, staffing and marketing.

D. Improved customer experience

EPOS can make shopping more convenient through faster checkouts, contactless payments, self-service checkouts, loyalty-card integration and accurate receipts.

💡 Example · Self-service checkout

A customer with only a few products can scan and pay for them at a self-service station without asking a cashier to scan each item.

2.4 Self-service checkout systems

A self-service checkout enables customers to scan products and complete payments with limited staff involvement.

✅ Potential benefits

  • Customers with a few items may finish more quickly.
  • Several checkout stations can be supervised by one employee.
  • Staff can be reassigned to other tasks.
  • Customers have more checkout choices.

⚠️ Potential drawbacks

  • Some customers find checkout machines difficult to use.
  • Technical problems require employee assistance.
  • Incorrect scanning or deliberate non-payment may cause losses.
  • Some customers prefer face-to-face service.
  • Traditional cashier duties may be reduced.
Important: Self-service checkouts do not benefit every customer equally. Accessibility needs, technical confidence and personal preferences may affect the experience.
Key point: EPOS links sales processing to inventory and business information, helping organisations make operational decisions.

3. Order Processing and ACID Principles

3.1 What is order processing?

KEY DEFINITION · Order Processing

Order processing is the management of transactions involved in receiving, validating, recording and completing customer orders.

Order processing is used by online shops, cinema booking systems, hotel reservation platforms and restaurant ordering systems. A system must ensure that orders are handled correctly and do not interfere with other orders.

💡 Example · Final ticket

If two customers try to buy the final cinema ticket, the system must prevent the same seat from being successfully allocated twice.

Reliable database transactions follow the ACID principles, which protect data integrity during changes.

3.2 What are the ACID principles?

AAtomicity

All or nothing

CConsistency

Valid data states

IIsolation

No interference

DDurability

Changes persist

These four properties help ensure that database transactions are handled accurately and reliably.

AtomicityConsistencyIsolationDurability

3.3 Atomicity – All or nothing

KEY DEFINITION · Atomicity

A transaction is treated as one complete, indivisible unit. Either all of its required operations succeed or its changes are not committed.

For example, transferring $200 from Account A to Account B requires two linked database operations:

  1. Deduct $200 from Account A.
  2. Add $200 to Account B.
Account A$1,000 → $800Money deducted
$200→
Account B$500 → $700Money added
What if an error occurs? If the amount is deducted from Account A but the credit to Account B fails, the transaction cannot be left partially committed. The database changes must be rolled back or otherwise resolved according to the transaction design.
Why atomicity matters: It prevents partially completed operations from creating incorrect balances, stock records or orders.

3.4 Consistency – Rules stay valid

KEY DEFINITION · Consistency

A transaction must move the database from one valid state to another valid state, preserving its required rules and constraints.

For instance, a system may check that a payment method is valid, a product is available, required information is present and stock records follow the organisation's rules.

💡 Example · Insufficient stock

An online store has 5 tablets, but a customer requests 8. If backorders are not allowed, the system should reject the purchase or require a smaller quantity. Allowing stock to become −3 would violate the rule prohibiting negative stock.

Why consistency matters: It keeps stored data accurate and makes sure established business rules are respected.

3.5 Isolation – Concurrent transactions do not interfere

KEY DEFINITION · Isolation

Transactions happening at the same time should not interfere with one another in ways that produce incorrect results.

This is important in multi-user systems where customers may attempt to buy the same limited item or book the same seat.

🎟️

Seat A12: only 1 available

Both Customer A and Customer B attempt to reserve it at nearly the same time.

↓
🔒

The system coordinates the bookings

Database concurrency controls prevent conflicting reservations.

↓
✅

Only one reservation succeeds

Customer A may receive confirmation while Customer B is told the seat is unavailable.

Why isolation matters: It prevents double-booking, overselling and incorrect updates when multiple users access the same data.

3.6 Durability – Saved changes persist

KEY DEFINITION · Durability

Once a transaction has been successfully committed, the recorded changes must remain stored, even if the system subsequently fails.

Imagine a customer completes an online order and receives confirmation. Shortly afterwards the server crashes. When the database recovers, the successfully committed order must still exist.

Reliable database storage and recovery mechanisms, such as transaction logs, help support durability.

💡 Example · Order confirmation

At 10:00 a.m., an order is committed. At 10:01 a.m., a server fails. After recovery, the confirmed order and its committed database changes remain available.

Why durability matters: It prevents completed transactions from disappearing after unexpected system failures.

3.7 Comparison of ACID principles

Principle Main question Practical example
Atomicity Did the whole transaction complete? Both sides of a bank transfer must complete.
Consistency Are the required rules still satisfied? Stock must not become negative if this is forbidden.
Isolation Did simultaneous transactions interfere? Two customers must not receive the same reserved seat.
Durability Will committed changes remain stored? A confirmed order must survive a server restart.
Remember: A = completeness, C = validity, I = concurrent operations, D = persistence.

3.8 What happens when ACID principles are not followed?

Failure Possible consequence
Atomicity failure A linked payment and order database operation remains incomplete.
Consistency failure Stock records violate required business rules.
Isolation failure Two customers receive the same cinema seat.
Durability failure A confirmed transaction disappears after a crash.
Key point: ACID is one of the most important technical concepts in order processing. Understand each principle and be able to apply it to a realistic transaction.

4. Financial Transaction Processing

4.1 What is financial transaction processing?

Financial transaction processing involves managing the movement and recording of money through information systems. Examples include bank transfers, card payments, salary payments, bill payments and online purchases.

These systems process important and sensitive information. An incorrect transaction can cause financial losses, delays or inaccurate account records. Therefore, accuracy, integrity, security and reliability are essential.

🧩

ACID compliance

Transactions preserve database integrity.

🕒

High availability

Systems stay accessible when needed.

📈

Scalability

Systems cope with increases in workload.

🛡️

Security

Financial information and operations are protected.

⚖️

Regulatory compliance

Applicable laws and regulations are followed.

4.2 ACID compliance

Financial database transactions should follow Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation and Durability to maintain correct and reliable account information.

  • All required database changes take place together.
  • Account data obeys required rules and constraints.
  • Concurrent transactions do not produce incorrect results.
  • Successfully committed transactions are stored reliably.
💡 Example · Online banking

A bank transfer must not lose its completed record if a server fails after the transaction is committed.

4.3 High availability

KEY DEFINITION · High Availability

High availability means designing a system to stay operational and accessible with as little downtime as possible.

Customers may need financial services at any hour, including nights and weekends. Banks therefore aim for 24/7 availability, supported by measures such as:

🖥️

Redundant servers

Alternative servers can take over when a primary server fails.

🔋

Backup power

Helps maintain operations when electrical power is interrupted.

🔁

Failover mechanisms

Switches services to working equipment when a failure occurs.

📊

Monitoring

Helps detect technical problems before they cause lengthy outages.

💡 Example · Banking service

If a banking server fails, a redundant server can take over so customers experience minimal interruption.

4.4 Scalability

KEY DEFINITION · Scalability

Scalability is the ability of a system to handle increasing workloads while maintaining acceptable performance.

Transaction volumes can increase during holidays, salary payment periods and major shopping events.

💡 Example · Sales campaign

A payment platform usually processes 2,000 transactions per hour, but a major sale increases demand to 15,000 transactions per hour. A scalable system provides additional capacity to handle the extra workload.

⬆️ Vertical scaling

Increase the CPU, memory or other resources of an existing server.

↔️ Horizontal scaling

Add more servers or processing resources to share the workload.

Extra technical explanation: Vertical and horizontal scaling are common approaches to achieving the scalability described in the textbook.

4.5 Security and fraud prevention

Financial systems handle sensitive data and must guard against unauthorised access, stolen information and fraudulent activity.

Security measure Purpose
Encryption Protects sensitive data by making it unreadable without the correct key.
Authentication Checks that users are who they claim to be.
Access controls Limits sensitive records and functions to authorised users.
Fraud detection Identifies suspicious or unusual transaction patterns.
💡 Example · Suspicious payment

A bank notices an unusually large payment and may request additional verification before allowing it to proceed.

4.6 Compliance with regulations

Financial institutions must follow applicable rules and laws to protect customers, support transparency and reduce financial crime.

  • Protect customer information.
  • Keep appropriate transaction records.
  • Reduce opportunities for financial crime.
  • Support authorised audits and investigations.
  • Operate financial systems responsibly.
💡 Example · Financial records

A financial institution may need to retain suitable transaction records for authorised reviews and audits.

4.7 Summary of financial system requirements

Requirement Meaning Purpose
ACID compliance Database transactions follow ACID properties. Maintain integrity.
High availability Services are accessible with minimal downtime. Provide dependable services.
Scalability Systems cope with growing transaction volumes. Maintain performance.
Security and fraud prevention Information and operations are protected. Reduce unauthorised access and fraud.
Regulatory compliance Systems follow applicable requirements. Support lawful, responsible operation.
Key point: Financial systems must be trustworthy not only when conditions are normal, but also during failures, high demand and attempted fraud.

5. Bacs Payment Schemes Limited

5.1 What is Bacs?

KEY DEFINITION · Bacs

Bacs Payment Schemes Limited (Bacs) is a UK-based payment scheme organisation associated with electronic payments between bank accounts, particularly direct debits and direct credits.

These payment arrangements support electronic transfers without requiring the exchange of cash. Two important types are direct debit and direct credit.

5.2 Direct debit

KEY DEFINITION · Direct Debit

A direct debit is an arrangement where a customer authorises an organisation to collect money from their bank account.

Direct debits are used for recurring payments, including electricity bills, water bills, mobile phone contracts, internet subscriptions and insurance premiums.

Customer's account💳Gives collection authorisation
Money collected→
Electricity company⚡Receives bill payment

The organisation initiates the collection under the customer's authorisation. The amount may be fixed or variable depending on the payment agreement.

Advantages of direct debit

  • Customers do not need to make each payment manually.
  • Payments can be collected regularly.
  • Organisations can automate recurring collections.
  • Customers are less likely to forget scheduled bills.

5.3 Direct credit

KEY DEFINITION · Direct Credit

A direct credit is a payment sent directly into a recipient's bank account by the payer.

Examples include salary payments, pensions, government benefits and payments from organisations to individuals.

Employer's account🏢Initiates salary payment
£2,000→
Employee's account💷Receives salary

Advantages of direct credit

  • Money is transferred electronically.
  • Organisations can automate large numbers of payments.
  • Employees do not need cash or physical cheques.
  • Electronic records make payments easier to track.

5.4 Differences between direct debit and direct credit

Feature Direct debit Direct credit
Who initiates? Receiving organisation (with authorisation) Payer
Direction of money Collected from the customer's account Sent into the recipient's account
Typical purpose Paying recurring bills Paying salaries or benefits
Example Internet provider collects a monthly bill Employer pays employees
Authorisation Customer permits a collection arrangement Payer approves payment to be sent
Easy way to remember: A direct debit is an authorised collection from your account. A direct credit is a payment into an account.

5.5 Similar payment systems in other countries

The textbook identifies systems in other regions with comparable electronic bank-payment functions:

Country or region System General function
United Kingdom Bacs Supports direct debit and direct credit arrangements.
United States Automated Clearing House (ACH) Supports electronic bank payments and transfers.
European participating countries Single Euro Payments Area (SEPA) Provides euro-denominated credit transfers and direct debit schemes.

These arrangements differ in geographical scope and operational rules but help individuals and organisations make electronic bank payments.

Key point: Distinguish direct debit from direct credit by identifying who initiates the transaction and which account receives the money.

6. Worked Example: A Supermarket Transaction

Consider a supermarket with an EPOS system connected to a stock database. A customer purchases five packets of rice and pays by card.

🛍️

Stage 1 · Sales processing

The EPOS system identifies the products, retrieves their prices and calculates the total.

↓
💳

Stage 2 · Payment processing

The card payment system attempts to authorise and process the payment securely.

↓
📦

Stage 3 · Inventory management

Following a successful sale, available stock is reduced by five packets.

↓
🧾

Stage 4 · Transaction recording

The details are saved and a receipt is provided to the customer.

↓
📊

Stage 5 · Business information

Managers later analyse sales records to understand demand and plan stock purchasing.

How ACID principles apply

ACID property Supermarket application
Atomicity A database transaction should not be left partially committed when linked operations fail.
Consistency Sales and stock records must follow the system's business rules.
Isolation Simultaneous purchases must not lead to incorrect stock quantities.
Durability Committed sales records must survive unexpected failures.
Important distinction: ACID primarily describes database transactions. A real card payment may be handled by a separate service, so synchronising payment outcomes with order and stock updates can require additional system logic.
Key point: A single checkout sale can involve EPOS, database transactions, inventory updates, payment processing and management information.
