Management Information Systems (MIS)

Understand how an MIS collects information from different sources, supports decision-making, improves project management and helps organisations operate efficiently.

📘 Detailed lesson notes🏭 Esho Shoes case study🥬 Grocery-store analysis activity
01 Getting started

Introduction to Management Information Systems

1.1 What is a Management Information System?

Key definition — MIS: A Management Information System is a computer-based system that collects, processes, stores and presents information from different sources to help managers operate an organisation effectively and make informed decisions.

An MIS combines information from different departments and sometimes from outside the organisation. Its main purpose is to turn large amounts of data into meaningful information that managers can use to improve organisational performance.

For example, a supermarket may use an EPOS system to record sales, a CRM system to store customer information, an inventory system to track stock and a finance system to record income and expenditure. An MIS can draw information from all these systems to give managers a clearer view of overall performance.

02 Core function

Record Keeping

2.1 What is record keeping in an MIS?

Record keeping involves collecting, organising, storing and maintaining data about an organisation's activities. An MIS can bring together information from different systems so managers do not need to examine many separate databases or reports.

2.2 Sources of data

A. Internal data sources

Internal data is generated by activities taking place inside the organisation.

Internal source Information collected Management use
EPOS Sales transactions, products, transaction times Identify popular products and sales trends
CRM Customer details, preferences, purchase history Improve customer service and marketing
Employee database Attendance, working hours, job roles, salaries Manage staffing and workforce requirements
Financial records Revenue, expenses, budgets, profits Monitor financial performance
Inventory system Stock levels, deliveries, reorder points Prevent shortages and overstocking
Operational systems Production output, downtime, delays Improve efficiency and productivity
Example — Clothing store: An EPOS system records jacket purchases. The MIS shows that winter jackets sell faster in November, helping the manager order enough stock for expected demand.

B. External data sources

External data is collected from outside the organisation and can provide context about markets, competitors and wider economic conditions.

External source Information provided Management use
Market research Customer needs and buying behaviour Identify opportunities for new products
Industry reports Trends and developments Understand industry changes
Government databases Economic, demographic and regulatory data Support business planning
Competitor analysis Competitor products, prices and promotions Improve pricing and marketing strategy
Supplier information Prices, availability and delivery schedules Plan purchasing and select suppliers
Example — Smartphone company: Internal sales records indicate a fall in sales of older models, while market research shows growing interest in longer battery life. Managers may use both types of data to guide product development.

2.3 Data processing in MIS

Data is not necessarily useful as soon as it is collected. An MIS may need to validate, cleanse and aggregate data before presenting reliable information.

✅

Validation

Checks data against predefined rules. Example: reject a negative stock quantity. Valid data is not necessarily factually correct.

🧹

Cleansing

Finds and corrects or removes incomplete, inaccurate, inconsistent or duplicate records.

📈

Aggregation

Combines many records into summaries, such as total daily sales or average weekly revenue.

Aggregation example

Instead of reading thousands of individual transactions, a manager can view a summary of daily sales:

2.4 Types of data handled by an MIS

Data type Meaning Example
Structured Organised in a predefined format, often database tables Sales table with product ID, price and quantity
Semi-structured Has some organisational structure but no fixed table format JSON and XML records
Unstructured Does not follow a predefined database structure Emails, photos, reports and free-text feedback
Remember: An MIS can work with all three types, but extracting useful meaning from unstructured information may require additional processing or analytical tools. Its role is to reduce complexity and present accessible information.
03 Core function

Decision-Making

3.1 How does MIS support decisions?

Managers must make decisions about staffing, budgets, production, purchasing, marketing and business strategy. An MIS supports these decisions by presenting high-quality information instead of leaving managers to rely only on assumptions.

Useful management information should be accurate, relevant, up to date, sufficiently complete, accessible to authorised users and easy to understand.

3.5 Levels of decision-making

Level Time horizon Example decision
Operational Short-term, daily activities How many staff should work tomorrow?
Tactical Medium-term department planning Should we increase orders for the next three months?
Strategic Long-term direction Should the company open another store?
Key point: MIS is especially useful for management reporting and tactical decisions, but its information can also support operational and strategic decisions.
04 Core function

Project Management

4.1 How does MIS support project management?

Project management involves planning, organising, monitoring and controlling resources to achieve defined objectives within time, cost and quality constraints.

An MIS can be integrated with project management software or used alongside it. Project management tools manage activities such as tasks, schedules and responsibilities; an MIS provides broader organisational information that helps managers determine whether projects are necessary, feasible and successful.

4.2 Uses of MIS in project management

Use Explanation Example
Identify opportunities Find business problems worth improving Production data reveals a slow process
Identify risks Reveal patterns that may cause project problems Supplier reports show repeated late deliveries
Allocate resources Show staffing, equipment and budget availability Add staff to an understaffed project
Monitor progress Compare actual performance with plans Report that spending exceeds the budget
Contingency planning Support alternative plans for possible disruptions Identify a backup supplier
Evaluate outcomes Compare results before and after the project Measure whether machine upgrades increased output

4.3 Example: Manufacturing improvement project

Collect operational evidence

The MIS combines production output, maintenance records, employee hours and equipment downtime.

Identify a problem

Analysis shows that one machine regularly breaks down and delays production.

Plan an improvement

Managers start a project to repair, upgrade or replace the machine.

Monitor and evaluate

MIS reports help compare project costs, delays and output before and after the work.

Difference to remember: Project management software primarily manages project tasks, schedules and resources. MIS provides the wider information needed to evaluate decisions and organisational performance. The two systems work well together.
05 Case Study

Case Study: Esho Shoes

5.1 Background

Esho Shoes manufactures shoes. The owner uses an MIS to identify opportunities to improve production efficiency. The MIS shows that one manufacturing line experiences frequent stoppages during shift changes.

5.2 Identifying the problem

Without the MIS, a manager might only see that the total number of shoes produced has fallen. For illustration, the factory might normally produce 1,000 pairs a day but now produces 850. This shows reduced output but does not explain the cause.

The MIS combines data on equipment downtime, production delays and operational inefficiencies. It reveals a recurring pattern: stoppages occur particularly often during shift changeovers.

5.3 Explanation of the findings

The MIS provides contextual information. Instead of knowing only that output has decreased, the manager can identify when and where problems occur and investigate their likely causes.

5.4 Possible implications and causes

  • Poor communication: Outgoing employees fail to explain unfinished tasks.
  • Equipment setup delays: Incoming staff need extra time to prepare machines.
  • Coordination problems: Responsibility for tasks is unclear.
  • Dependence on individuals: Only certain workers can complete critical tasks.
  • Maintenance interruptions: Servicing coincides with shift changes.
Important: These are possible explanations, not automatically proven causes. The manager should investigate further rather than assuming the MIS has established the cause.

5.5 Actions taken by management

🤝

Improve handovers

Use clearer shift-change procedures, handover notes and better communication between teams.

🎓

Cross-train staff

Train employees to perform several roles, reducing reliance on particular individuals.

🔧

Adjust maintenance

Schedule maintenance more effectively to reduce unexpected production downtime.

5.6 Impact of MIS

Without MIS With MIS
Manager sees reduced output only Manager identifies downtime associated with shift changes
Root causes are harder to investigate Manager can focus on likely causes
Decisions may depend more on guesswork Actions can be supported by detailed evidence
Improvements might target the wrong area Training, maintenance and handovers can be reviewed
Problems may take longer to resolve Potential solutions may be identified more quickly
Case study conclusion: The MIS helps Esho Shoes find a more specific pattern behind reduced output. Better-focused improvements may save time and money, but results should be checked using later performance data.
06 Applied activity

Activity 5: Marius's Grocery Stores

6.1 Scenario

Marius owns five grocery stores across a large city. The stores sell fresh fruit and vegetables and use EPOS systems to record sales. Marius notices that one store wastes significantly more fresh produce than the other four.

Activity question: Analyse how Marius could use the EPOS system and the data it provides to discover why one store wastes more fresh produce than the others.

6.2 Step-by-step analysis

Compare sales across all five stores

Examine the amount and type of produce sold, and compare daily and weekly demand. A particular branch may sell fewer strawberries or leafy vegetables than the others.

Compare deliveries and actual sales

Combine EPOS records with inventory and delivery data. If a store receives 200 packs of strawberries but sells only 120 before they spoil, it may be ordering too much.

Look for sales patterns

Check when different products sell most frequently. A branch may receive large Monday deliveries even though most demand occurs at weekends.

Identify the products with the most waste

Compare sales with stock and wastage records to calculate waste rates and locate the main problem products.

Investigate other possible causes

Check stock rotation, storage temperatures, pricing, promotions and the accuracy of demand forecasts.

Take action and evaluate

Adjust order quantities, delivery frequency and stock handling. Continue reviewing data to see whether the waste rate decreases.

6.4 Other possible causes of waste

Possible cause Evidence to investigate
Overstocking Deliveries repeatedly exceed customer demand
Poor stock rotation Older products are left unsold while newer products are displayed first
Poor storage conditions Temperature logs show unsuitable conditions
Uncompetitive prices Customers buy fewer products due to higher prices
Weak forecasting Orders are based on inaccurate demand estimates
Ineffective promotions Produce remains unsold despite available stock

6.5 Corrective actions

  • Reduce order quantities for products with consistently low demand.
  • Arrange smaller, more frequent deliveries.
  • Use historical EPOS data to improve demand forecasts.
  • Discount suitable products approaching their sell-by dates, where appropriate.
  • Improve storage conditions and first-in, first-out stock rotation.
  • Train staff in handling and monitoring fresh produce.
Crucial distinction: EPOS data records what is sold. It cannot by itself prove how much produce has spoiled or why. For a reliable explanation, Marius should also use inventory, delivery, wastage and storage records, plus staff observations.

6.6 Complete analytical answer

Model response

Marius could use the EPOS system to analyse sales data for fresh fruit and vegetables across all five stores. Comparing the number and types of products sold would help him identify whether the branch with the greatest wastage has lower demand for particular products.

He could also examine sales patterns on different days to determine whether stock deliveries and order quantities match customer demand. For example, if large quantities of fresh produce are delivered but only a small proportion is sold before spoilage, the store may be overstocking.

By combining EPOS data with inventory, delivery and wastage records, Marius could compare the amount purchased, sold and discarded. This would allow him to calculate waste rates and identify the products that contribute most to the problem.

He should then investigate other possible causes, such as poor storage, ineffective stock rotation or inaccurate demand forecasts. Marius could use his findings to change order quantities, improve delivery schedules and introduce suitable promotions.

These actions could reduce wasted stock and financial losses. By continuing to monitor sales and wastage figures, he could assess whether the changes improved profitability.

08 Evaluation

Limitations and Challenges of MIS

⚠️

Data quality

Incorrect or out-of-date records can produce misleading reports and poor decisions.

💸

Implementation costs

Software, hardware, integration, maintenance and staff training can be expensive.

🔐

Security and privacy

Sensitive financial, employee and customer information must be protected against unauthorised access.

💻

Technology dependence

Failures and outages may prevent access to vital information. Backups and recovery plans are needed.

🌊

Information overload

Too much poorly organised information can hide important findings and make decisions harder.

🧑‍💼

Human judgement

MIS supports decisions, but managers must investigate causes and interpret information carefully.

Example: A report shows falling sales. The cause might be competition, seasonal demand, changing preferences or an internal problem. The MIS shows the trend; managers must investigate before choosing an action.
09 Essential understanding

Important Distinctions

9.1 MIS compared with EPOS and CRM

System Main purpose
EPOS Records sales transactions and provides sales information
CRM Manages customer information, interactions and relationships
MIS Combines and analyses information from multiple sources to support management decisions
Example: EPOS records a shoe sale; CRM may store the customer's purchase history; MIS can combine sales, customer, stock and financial information to support next month's purchasing decisions.

9.2 Data, information and knowledge

Term Meaning Example
Data Raw facts or figures 120, 150, 130, 100
Information Processed data given context and meaning A report shows sales have decreased
Knowledge Understanding gained by interpreting information Manager identifies a likely cause and appropriate response
Remember: Data becomes valuable when it is processed, interpreted in context and used to inform appropriate actions.

Overall conclusion

A Management Information System helps turn organisational data into useful information. By combining internal and external sources, it enables managers to identify patterns, understand problems, monitor performance and make better-informed decisions.

Central idea: MIS supports management decisions with evidence rather than assumptions, while human judgement remains necessary to interpret the findings.
